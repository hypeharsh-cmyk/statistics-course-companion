Part 1

Hypothesis testing

By the end of this part you will be able to take any hypothesis-testing question in this course, fill in the same six boxes, and write a complete answer without deciding anything on the fly.

Every hypothesis test in this syllabus is the same procedure. Only three things change from question to question: which formula goes on top, which table you look in, and how many tails. Everything else — the order of the steps, the words in the conclusion — never changes. So the goal here is not to understand six tests. It is to learn one machine and six settings.

In plain English

A hypothesis test is an argument by contradiction. You assume the boring thing is true ("the coin is fair", "the variance really is 20"), you work out how surprising your data would be if that were true, and if the answer is "very surprising indeed", you stop believing the boring thing. That is all. The formulas just measure "surprising" in a standard unit.

1.1 · The machine — six steps, every single time

Write these six lines down before you look at the numbers. Leave gaps. Then fill them in. Examiners give marks for the steps, not only the final number, and the instruction on your paper is explicit — "answer all the questions with detailed steps in a proper order without any kind of step jump." A correct answer with three steps missing loses more marks than a wrong answer with all six present.

The six-step skeleton — copy this into your answer script
  1. State the hypotheses.

    $H_0$ and $H_1$, in symbols, about a population parameter ($\mu$, $p$, $\sigma^2$) — never about $\bar{x}$, $\hat{p}$ or $s^2$. Note which one has the equals sign: that is always $H_0$.

  2. State the level of significance and the tail.

    Write $\alpha = 0.05$ (or whatever is given) and the words "left-tailed", "right-tailed" or "two-tailed". The tail comes from the direction of $H_1$ — nothing else.

  3. Name the test statistic and write its formula.

    One line, un-substituted. This is the step that earns marks for knowing which test it is.

  4. Find the critical value, or the rejection region.

    State the table, the degrees of freedom, and the value you read off, e.g. "from the $t$-table with $df = 8$, $t_{0.05,8} = 1.860$". Do this before you compute, so you cannot be accused of choosing it to suit your answer.

  5. Compute the test statistic.

    Full substitution, then the number. Four decimal places, as the paper asks.

  6. Decide, then conclude in words.

    Compare, state "reject $H_0$" or "do not reject $H_0$", then one sentence in the language of the original question. Underline it.

Steps 1, 2, 3 and 4 can all be written before you touch the calculator. That is usually half the marks.

1.2 · Step 1 — turning the English into H₀ and H₁

This is where most marks are lost, and it is pure translation. Two rules settle every case:

The two rules

Rule 1 — the equals sign lives in $H_0$. $H_0$ always contains $=$, $\ge$ or $\le$. $H_1$ never contains an equals sign. If you have written $H_1: \mu = 50$ you have made a mistake.

Rule 2 — $H_1$ is what you would need evidence to believe. $H_0$ is the status quo, the claim, the "no change" position. You only abandon it under pressure.

Here is the phrase book. Find the wording of your question in the left column.

The trap in "test the claim"

When a question says "test the claim of the manufacturer", the claim goes into $H_0$ — because the claim is the thing being defended, and the test asks whether the data forces us to abandon it. Rejecting $H_0$ then means the claim is not supported. Students frequently put the claim into $H_1$ and end up writing the opposite conclusion to the one their own arithmetic supports.

1.3 · Step 3 — choosing the test statistic

Read the question for three words only: what is being tested (a mean, a proportion, a variance), and if it is a mean, is $\sigma$ known. That is the entire decision.

  • What parameter does the question ask about?
    • A mean $\mu$ — "average", "mean lifetime", "mean weight"
      • Is the population $\sigma$ given?
        • Yes $\rightarrow$ $Z$ test, $\;z = \dfrac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}}$, use the $Z$-table
        • No, only the sample $s$ (or raw data) $\rightarrow$ $t$ test, $\;t = \dfrac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}}$, $df = n-1$
    • A proportion $p$ — "percentage", "% conforming", "fraction defective", a count out of $n$
      • $\rightarrow$ $Z$ test for a proportion, $\;z = \dfrac{\hat{p}-p_0}{\sqrt{p_0(1-p_0)/n}}$
    • A variance $\sigma^2$ or a standard deviation $\sigma$ — "variance", "variability", "consistency", "spread"
      • $\rightarrow$ $\chi^2$ test, $\;\chi^2 = \dfrac{(n-1)s^2}{\sigma_0^2}$, $df = n-1$
Exam technique

The giveaway for a proportion test is a count: "164 out of 200", "36 of 400 were defective". The giveaway for a variance test is the word variance or a target spread, e.g. "is the population variance equal to 20 square kgs". The giveaway for a $t$ test is being handed raw data with no $\sigma$ — because then you must compute $s$ yourself, and the moment you compute $s$ you are in $t$ territory.

The three formulas, side by side
$$ z=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{\sigma/\sqrt{n}} \qquad t=\frac{\bar{x}-\mu_0}{s/\sqrt{n}} \qquad z=\frac{\hat{p}-p_0}{\sqrt{\dfrac{p_0(1-p_0)}{n}}} \qquad \chi^2=\frac{(n-1)s^2}{\sigma_0^2} $$
Notice the shape is identical in the first three: (what I saw) − (what H0 claims), divided by the standard error. The $\chi^2$ is the odd one out because variances are ratios, not differences — you divide instead of subtracting.
The commonest slip in the proportion test

The denominator uses $p_0$ — the value from $H_0$ — not $\hat{p}$ from the sample. You are asking "how would $\hat{p}$ behave if $H_0$ were true", so everything about the null world comes from $H_0$. (A confidence interval for $p$ is different: there, with no null to assume, you use $\hat{p}$. Same symbol, different job.)

1.4 · Step 4 — finding the critical value

Three questions, in this order: which table, how many tails, how many degrees of freedom.

Splitting $\alpha$ — why 5% becomes 2.5%

A two-tailed test is suspicious of departures in both directions, so the 5% of "surprising" outcomes has to be shared out, 2.5% at each end. That is why the two-tailed 5% critical value is $z_{0.025}=1.96$ and not $z_{0.05}=1.645$. One-tailed tests put the whole 5% at one end. Students who forget to halve $\alpha$ reject far too often.

Reading the $\chi^2$ table — the one that catches everybody

The $\chi^2$ table is labelled by upper-tail area. The column headed $\chi^2_{0.050}$ holds the value with 5% of the area to its right. So for a two-tailed test at 5% you need both $\chi^2_{0.975}$ (the small one, on the left) and $\chi^2_{0.025}$ (the big one, on the right). The $\chi^2$ curve is not symmetric, so — unlike $Z$ and $t$ — you cannot get the left value by putting a minus sign on the right one. You must look up two separate numbers.

1.5 · Deciding — two routes that always agree

There are two ways to finish, and they always agree. Use whichever the question names; if it names neither, the critical-value route is faster.

In plain English — what a p-value is

The p-value is the answer to: "if $H_0$ were true, what fraction of samples would look at least this extreme?" A p-value of 0.0414 says only about 4 samples in 100 would be this far out by luck alone. A p-value of 0.40 says 40 in 100 would — so nothing about your sample is surprising, and you have no case against $H_0$. Small p, reject. Say it to yourself as "small p means surprising means the null takes the blame".

1.6 · The p-value method, in detail

Because one full question on the May 2026 paper demanded it by name, this deserves its own recipe. The only new skill is converting a test statistic into a tail area — and then doubling it, or not.

Computing a p-value
  1. Compute the test statistic as usual.

    Nothing changes about steps 1–5.

  2. Look up the area in the tail your $H_1$ points at.

    Left-tailed $H_1$ ($<$) $\rightarrow$ area to the left of your $z$: read $\Phi(z)$ straight off the table.
    Right-tailed $H_1$ ($>$) $\rightarrow$ area to the right: $1 - \Phi(z)$.

  3. If the test is two-tailed, double it.

    $p = 2 \times P(Z > |z|)$. Two-tailed means "extreme in either direction", so both tails count.

  4. Compare with $\alpha$ and state the comparison explicitly.

    Write the inequality out — "$0.0001 < 0.01$" — then the decision. The comparison itself carries marks.

With $t$ and $\chi^2$ you cannot get an exact p-value — bracket it instead

The $Z$-table gives areas, so a $Z$ p-value is exact. The $t$ and $\chi^2$ tables give only a handful of critical values, so you can only trap the p-value between two columns. That is a perfectly good exam answer, and is what is expected. For example, with $t = 2.8823$ on $df = 8$: the table row reads $t_{0.025,8} = 2.306$ and $t_{0.01,8} = 2.896$. Your value sits between them, so $0.01 < p < 0.025$. Since $p < 0.05$, reject at 5%. Write it exactly like that.

1.7 · The conclusion sentence — a template you can memorise

The final mark is for saying what happened in the language of the question, not in the language of statistics. Two templates cover everything.

When you reject:
Since test statistic falls in the rejection region (or p < α), we reject $H_0$ at the α×100% level. There is sufficient evidence to conclude that $H_1$ in the question's own words.
When you do not reject:
Since test statistic does not fall in the rejection region (or p > α), we do not reject $H_0$ at the α×100% level. There is insufficient evidence to conclude that $H_1$ in the question's own words.
Never write "we accept $H_0$"

Failing to convict is not the same as proving innocence. A small sample can fail to detect a real effect, so "do not reject" means only "this data was not enough". Write "do not reject $H_0$" or "there is insufficient evidence". Examiners deduct for "accept".

1.8 · The real thing — exam Q5, broken apart

May 2026 end-term · Question 5 · 7 marks · testing a proportion by the p-value method

Now watch the machine run on the actual paper. Every step below is labelled with the step of the recipe it comes from, so you can see there is nothing new happening — only the six boxes being filled.

Exam Question 5 7 marks

A manufacturer claimed that at least 90% of the components which he supplied are conforming to the specifications. However, a random sample of 200 components showed only 164 were conforming to the specifications. Test the claim of the manufacturer at 1% level of significance by using p-value method.

Reading the question before any maths

Three things to extract, and one instruction to obey:

  • What is being tested? "90% of the components" and a count "164 of 200" — this is a proportion. So the $Z$ test for a proportion.
  • What is the claim? "at least 90%", i.e. $p \ge 0.90$. And it is the manufacturer's claim, so it goes in $H_0$.
  • What is $\alpha$? 1%, so $\alpha = 0.01$.
  • What method? The question says p-value method. You must show a p-value; a critical-value answer alone will not score full marks.
Given $n = 200$ · $x = 164$ · $p_0 = 0.90$ · $\alpha = 0.01$
Step 1 state the hypotheses

The claim carries the equals sign, so it is $H_0$. The alternative is what would disprove the claim: that the true conforming rate is below 90%.

$$ H_0: p \ge 0.90 \qquad\text{against}\qquad H_1: p < 0.90 $$

(Writing $H_0: p = 0.90$ is also accepted — the test is computed at the boundary either way.)

Step 2 significance level and tail

$\alpha = 0.01$. Because $H_1$ points left ($<$), this is a left-tailed test. That single fact decides the whole of the final step, so write it down now.

Step 3 name the statistic

Sample proportion first, because everything else needs it:

$$ \hat{p}=\frac{x}{n}=\frac{164}{200}=0.82 $$

Then the test statistic, un-substituted:

$$ z=\frac{\hat{p}-p_0}{\sqrt{\dfrac{p_0(1-p_0)}{n}}} $$

Check the sample is large enough for the normal approximation: $np_0 = 200(0.90) = 180 \ge 5$ and $n(1-p_0) = 200(0.10) = 20 \ge 5$. Both comfortably pass, so $Z$ is valid. One line, and it is a mark.

Step 4 the p-value method replaces the critical value

With the p-value method you do not need a critical value — but it costs nothing to note that a left-tailed test at 1% would reject below $-z_{0.01} = -2.326$, and it lets you sanity-check the final decision.

Step 5 compute

Denominator first, slowly — this is where arithmetic slips happen:

$$ \sqrt{\frac{0.90 \times 0.10}{200}}=\sqrt{\frac{0.09}{200}}=\sqrt{0.00045}=0.0212 $$

Then the statistic:

$$ z=\frac{0.82-0.90}{0.0212132}=\frac{-0.08}{0.0212132}=-3.7712 $$
Step 6a the p-value

$H_1$ points left, so the p-value is the area to the left of $-3.7712$ — no doubling, because this is a one-tailed test.

$$ p\text{-value}=P(Z < -3.7712) \approx 0.0001 $$

Printed $Z$-tables usually stop around $z = -3.49$, where the area is $0.0002$. Since $-3.7712$ lies even further into the tail, the area is smaller still. Write "$p\text{-value} < 0.0002$, approximately $0.0001$" — that is honest and fully creditable.

Step 6b decide and conclude

Compare, explicitly:

$$ p\text{-value} = 0.0001 \;<\; \alpha = 0.01 $$
Reject $H_0$. At the 1% level there is sufficient evidence that fewer than 90% of components conform, so the manufacturer's claim is not supported.

Sanity check against the other route: $z = -3.7712 < -2.326$, which is deep inside the rejection region. Same decision, as it must be.

Why this one is worth 7 marks

Because it hides three separate judgements: recognising a proportion from a count, putting the manufacturer's claim in $H_0$ rather than $H_1$, and using $p_0$ — not $\hat p$ — inside the square root. Get those three right and the arithmetic is one line.

1.9 · The real thing — exam Q6, broken apart

May 2026 end-term · Question 6 · 6 marks · testing a variance with $\chi^2$

Exam Question 6 6 marks

Weights (in kgs) of 10 students are given below: 38, 40, 45, 53, 47, 43, 55, 48, 52, 49. Can we say that the population variance of the distribution of weights of all students from which the above sample of 10 students was drawn is equal to 20 square kgs? Test an appropriate hypothesis at 5% level.

Reading the question
  • "population variance … equal to 20" — a variance test, so $\chi^2$.
  • "equal to" — no direction is suggested, so this is two-tailed.
  • Raw data is given and no $\sigma$, so you must compute $s^2$ yourself.
  • "Test an appropriate hypothesis" is the examiner telling you to choose the test — which is part of what is being marked.
Given $n = 10$ · $\sigma_0^2 = 20$ · $\alpha = 0.05$ · raw data
Step 1 & 2 hypotheses, level, tail
$$ H_0: \sigma^2 = 20 \qquad\text{against}\qquad H_1: \sigma^2 \ne 20 $$

$\alpha = 0.05$, two-tailed, $df = n - 1 = 9$.

Step 3 the statistic
$$ \chi^2=\frac{(n-1)s^2}{\sigma_0^2}\;\sim\;\chi^2_{n-1} \quad\text{where}\quad s^2=\frac{\sum (x-\bar{x})^2}{n-1} $$
Step 4 two critical values, because two tails

From the $\chi^2$ table with $df = 9$, splitting $\alpha$ into 2.5% at each end:

$$ \chi^2_{0.975,\,9}=2.700 \qquad\text{and}\qquad \chi^2_{0.025,\,9}=19.023 $$

So the rule is: reject $H_0$ if $\chi^2 < 2.700$ or $\chi^2 > 19.023$; otherwise do not reject. Remember the table is labelled by right-tail area, which is why the lower critical value sits in the column headed $\chi^2_{0.975}$.

Step 5a build the deviation table

First the mean. $\sum x = 38+40+45+53+47+43+55+48+52+49 = 470$, so

$$ \bar{x}=\frac{470}{10}=47 $$

A whole-number mean is a gift — the deviations stay clean. Tabulate them:

$x$38404553474355485249Σ
$x-\bar{x}$−9−7−260−481520
$(x-\bar{x})^2$8149436016641254280

The deviations summing to $0$ is your free arithmetic check — if that row does not total zero, your mean is wrong and everything after it will be too.

Step 5b sample variance, then the statistic
$$ s^2=\frac{280}{10-1}=\frac{280}{9}=31.1111 $$

Now substitute. Notice the $n-1$ cancels against the $n-1$ inside $s^2$, so in practice $\chi^2 = \sum(x-\bar{x})^2 / \sigma_0^2$ — a useful shortcut and a good check:

$$ \chi^2=\frac{(10-1)(31.1111)}{20}=\frac{280}{20}=14.0000 $$
Step 6 decide and conclude
$$ 2.700 \;<\; 14.0000 \;<\; 19.023 $$

The statistic lands between the two critical values, i.e. outside both rejection regions.

Do not reject $H_0$. At the 5% level there is insufficient evidence that the population variance differs from 20 square kgs, so we may take it to be 20.
If you are asked to bracket the p-value too

Look along the $df = 9$ row for $14.0000$: it lies between $\chi^2_{0.100,9}=14.684$ and $\chi^2_{0.250}$ (off this table). Since $14.0000 < 14.684$, the right-tail area exceeds $0.10$, so for a two-tailed test $p > 0.20$ — comfortably above $0.05$, confirming the same decision.

1.10 · The mistakes that actually cost marks

  • Hypotheses about the sample. $H_0: \bar{x} = 47$ is meaningless — you know $\bar{x}$. Hypotheses are always about the population: $\mu$, $p$, $\sigma^2$.
  • Forgetting to halve $\alpha$. Two-tailed at 5% is $1.96$, not $1.645$. This single slip flips a decision surprisingly often.
  • Using $\hat{p}$ in the standard error. In a test it is $p_0$ under the square root, every time.
  • Dividing by $n$ instead of $n-1$ when computing $s^2$ from raw data. The test needs the sample variance with $n-1$.
  • Taking the left $\chi^2$ value as minus the right one. $\chi^2$ is not symmetric and is never negative. Two tails means two separate lookups.
  • Writing "accept $H_0$". Say "do not reject".
  • Stopping at the number. No sentence in the question's own words means a lost mark, no matter how perfect the arithmetic.
  • Rounding too early. Carry the full calculator value into the next step and round only the final answer, to four decimals as the paper instructs.

Part 1 recap

  • One machine, six steps, written before the numbers: hypotheses · level and tail · statistic · critical value · compute · conclude.
  • $H_0$ owns the equals sign and owns the claim. $H_1$ sets the tail.
  • Mean with $\sigma$ $\rightarrow$ $Z$ · mean without $\sigma$ $\rightarrow$ $t$, $df=n-1$ · proportion $\rightarrow$ $Z$ with $p_0$ inside the root · variance $\rightarrow$ $\chi^2$, $df=n-1$, two lookups if two-tailed.
  • p-value route: area in the tail $H_1$ points at, doubled if two-tailed; reject when $p \le \alpha$. With $t$ and $\chi^2$, bracket it between two columns.
  • Finish with a sentence in the question's own words, and underline it.
