Chapter 4

Hypothesis Testing

By the end of this chapter you can set up H₀ and Hₐ correctly, choose the right test statistic, find the rejection region, compute a p-value, and write the conclusion in the words an examiner wants.

Chapter 3 asked "what is the parameter, roughly?" This chapter asks a sharper question: "someone claims the parameter is 50 — does my sample give me the right to call that claim wrong?" It is a formal procedure for disagreeing with a number, and it has a fixed shape you can learn once and reuse for means, proportions and variances alike.

4.1 · What a hypothesis is

Definition A hypothesis is a claim or statement about a numerical property of a population — a population parameter such as the mean $\mu$, the proportion $P$, or the variance $\sigma^2$.
Notes from the slides — types of hypotheses
  • Research hypotheses: an assertion or conjecture about a population parameter arising from practical, scientific, or managerial questions.
  • Statistical hypotheses: formal statements about population parameters expressed in mathematical terms ($H_0$ and $H_1$).
  • Examples of research hypotheses: a new drug is more effective than the current market standard; the mean assembly time for a new process is less than 50 minutes; the proportion of defective items produced by a machine is less than 0.03.
  • Statistical hypotheses are stated in terms of population parameters, not sample statistics, and are evaluated using sample data to determine whether there is sufficient evidence to reject the initial assumption.

The two hypotheses

Notes from the slides
  • Null Hypothesis ($H_0$): a statement of no difference, no effect, or status quo. It states the belief or baseline assumption about a population parameter. It is assumed to be true until proven otherwise, and may be rejected or failed to be rejected based on sample evidence.
  • Alternative Hypothesis ($H_a$ or $H_1$): the opposite of the null. It represents what the researcher tries to support or gather evidence for — it carries the burden of proof.
  • Rule for the equality sign: the equality sign ($=$, $\le$, or $\ge$) ALWAYS belongs in the null hypothesis. The alternative hypothesis NEVER contains an equality sign — it uses $\neq$, $>$, or $<$.
  • $H_0$ refers to a specified parameter value, not a sample statistic. Example: $H_0: \mu = 100$ is correct; $H_0: \bar{X} = 100$ is incorrect.
The courtroom, which is the whole idea in one picture $H_0$ is "the defendant is innocent". We start by assuming it, not because we believe it, but because it is the position that must be disproved. The prosecution ($H_a$) carries the burden of proof. If the evidence is weak we do not declare the defendant innocent — we say "not guilty", meaning we failed to prove otherwise. That is exactly the difference between rejecting $H_0$ and failing to reject it.
Never write "accept H₀"

The two legal verdicts are guilty and not guilty — never innocent. Likewise your two statistical verdicts are reject $H_0$ and fail to reject $H_0$.

"Fail to reject" means the evidence was not strong enough, which is not at all the same as proving $H_0$ true. Writing "accept $H_0$" is one of the few phrases that reliably loses marks even when every number above it is right.

Setting up the hypotheses — a method that always works
  1. Find the claim being tested or challenged, and write it with an equals sign. That is your $H_0$.
  2. Ask what the question wants evidence for. That is your $H_a$.
  3. Check: does $H_a$ contain $\neq$, $>$ or $<$? Does $H_0$ carry the equality? If not, you have them the wrong way round.

Example from the slides — testing if the population mean equals 50:   $H_0: \mu = 50$   versus   $H_a: \mu \neq 50$.

4.2 · One-tailed and two-tailed tests

Notes from the slides
  • Two-tailed test: used when $H_a$ specifies a non-directional difference ($\neq$). The rejection region is divided into both tails of the sampling distribution.
  • One-tailed test: used when $H_a$ specifies a directional difference ($>$ or $<$). The rejection region is located entirely in one tail — right-tail for $>$, left-tail for $<$.
TypeHypothesesCritical regionp-value
Right / upper tail$H_0: \mu \le \mu_0$ vs $H_a: \mu > \mu_0$$Z > Z_\alpha$$P(Z > Z_{calc})$
Left / lower tail$H_0: \mu \ge \mu_0$ vs $H_a: \mu < \mu_0$$Z < -Z_\alpha$$P(Z < Z_{calc})$
Two tail$H_0: \mu = \mu_0$ vs $H_a: \mu \neq \mu_0$$Z < -Z_{\alpha/2}$ or $Z > Z_{\alpha/2}$$2 \times P(Z > |Z_{calc}|)$
The α/2 trap A two-tailed test splits α between the two ends, so each tail gets only α/2. At $\alpha = 0.05$ the two-tailed critical value is 1.96, but the one-tailed value is 1.645. Using 1.645 for a two-tailed test makes you reject far too easily. Decide which test you are running before you look up any value.

4.3 · The six steps

Steps in Testing Hypotheses — from the slides
  1. State the null hypothesis ($H_0$) and the alternative hypothesis ($H_a$).
  2. Select the level of significance ($\alpha$) and sample size ($n$).
  3. Determine the appropriate test statistic and sampling distribution.
  4. Determine the critical value(s) and define the decision rule (or rejection region).
  5. Collect sample data, compute the sample statistic and the test statistic value.
  6. Make the statistical decision (reject $H_0$ or fail to reject $H_0$) and state the conclusion in managerial terms.
Note — the hypothesis testing process Assume $H_0$ is true → take a random sample → compute the test statistic → compare the test statistic with the critical value (or the p-value with $\alpha$) → make the decision to reject or not reject $H_0$.
Write out all six steps in the exam Structured method marks are the easiest marks in this chapter. Even if your arithmetic slips, a visible $H_0$/$H_a$, a stated α, a named test statistic, a stated critical value and a written conclusion will collect most of the credit. Step 6 in words — about the actual pizzas or batteries, not about "the null hypothesis" — is the one students most often skip.

4.4 · Significance, the rejection region, and why we reject

Notes from the slides
  • Reason for rejecting $H_0$: if the sample statistic is unlikely to occur given that $H_0$ is true, we reject $H_0$. "Unlikely" means the test statistic falls in the critical/rejection region, where the probability of occurrence under $H_0$ is very small ($\le \alpha$).
  • Level of significance, $\alpha$: defines the probability of making a Type I error (rejecting $H_0$ when $H_0$ is true). Commonly chosen values are $\alpha = 0.05$, $0.01$ or $0.10$. It is set by the researcher prior to data collection.
  • Critical region (rejection region): the set of values of the test statistic for which the null hypothesis is rejected.
  • Non-rejection region: the set of values for which $H_0$ is not rejected.
  • Critical value(s): the boundary value(s) separating the rejection region from the non-rejection region.
The logic, in one sentence Assume the claim is true, then ask: if it really were true, how freakish would my sample be? If the answer is "so freakish it would happen less than 5% of the time", we stop believing the claim. It is proof by contradiction, run on probabilities instead of certainties.
α is chosen BEFORE you see the data Picking α after seeing your test statistic — nudging it from 0.05 to 0.10 so the result "works" — destroys the meaning of the whole procedure. The slides are explicit: α is set by the researcher prior to data collection.

4.5 · The two errors, and the power of a test

Notes from the slides
  • Type I error: occurs when a true null hypothesis is rejected. Probability $= \alpha$.
  • Type II error: occurs when a false null hypothesis is not rejected. Probability $= \beta$.
  • You cannot commit both errors simultaneously in a single decision.
  • Reducing $\alpha$ increases $\beta$ for a fixed sample size $n$.
  • Increasing sample size $n$ reduces both $\alpha$ and $\beta$ risks.

Outcomes and Probabilities

Decision$H_0$ is true$H_0$ is false ($H_a$ true)
Do not reject $H_0$Correct decision  ($1-\alpha$)Type II error  ($\beta$)
Reject $H_0$Type I error  ($\alpha$)Correct decision — Power  ($1-\beta$)
Two ways to be wrong, in plain words Type I is a false alarm: the fire alarm goes off with no fire. Type II is a miss: there is a fire and the alarm stays silent. Make the alarm more sensitive and you get fewer misses but more false alarms. Make it less sensitive and the reverse. The only way to improve both at once is a better detector — which in statistics means a bigger sample.
The courtroom example, from the slides

$H_0$: the defendant is innocent  vs  $H_a$: the defendant is guilty.

  • Type I error: convicting an innocent person (rejecting a true $H_0$).
  • Type II error: acquitting a guilty person (failing to reject a false $H_0$).
  • The judicial system prioritises minimising Type I error — protecting innocent people — by setting a very high standard of proof ("beyond a reasonable doubt"), and accepts a higher Type II error risk as the price.
  • In industrial quality control the same trade-off appears as producer's risk (Type I — rejecting a good batch) versus consumer's risk (Type II — accepting a bad batch).
Power of the test
$$ \text{Power} = 1 - \beta = P(\text{Reject } H_0 \mid H_0 \text{ is false}) $$
β the probability of a Type II error · Power the probability of correctly rejecting a false null — a test's ability to detect a real effect
Note from the slides Power increases as the sample size $n$ increases, as $\alpha$ increases, or as the true parameter moves further from the hypothesised value. There is an inverse relationship between $\alpha$ and $\beta$: as $\alpha$ decreases, $\beta$ increases and vice versa, assuming the sample size $n$ remains constant.

4.6 · The p-value approach

Notes from the slides
  • p-value: the probability of obtaining a test statistic as extreme as, or more extreme than, the observed value, assuming $H_0$ is true. Also known as the observed level of significance.
  • Decision rule: reject $H_0$ if p-value $< \alpha$; fail to reject $H_0$ if p-value $\ge \alpha$.
In plain English The p-value answers: "if the claim were true, how often would I see a sample at least this extreme?" A p-value of 0.002 means "twice in a thousand" — so either something very unusual happened, or the claim is wrong. A p-value of 0.40 means "four times in ten", which is nothing remarkable at all.
"If p is low, H₀ must go" Compare the p-value directly against α. Small p, reject. And note how the tail affects the computation: a two-tailed p-value is doubled, because "as extreme" includes both directions.
What a p-value is NOT It is not the probability that $H_0$ is true. It is a probability computed assuming $H_0$ is true — you cannot turn it around. Nor does a small p-value mean the effect is large; with a big enough sample, a trivially small difference can produce a tiny p-value.

The two methods below always give the same verdict. Drag α and the test statistic and watch both readouts move together — the marker crossing into the shaded zone happens at exactly the moment the p-value drops below α.

4.7 · Testing a mean when σ is known — the Z test

Assumptions Population standard deviation $\sigma$ is known · the population is normally distributed, OR the sample size $n \ge 30$ (by the Central Limit Theorem).
Z test for a mean, σ² known
$$ Z = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu_0}{\dfrac{\sigma}{\sqrt{n}}} $$
x̄ the sample mean · μ₀ the value claimed in H₀ · σ/√n the standard error · degrees of freedom: not applicable, $Z \sim N(0,1)$
Decision rule — critical value approach
  • Two-tailed ($H_a: \mu \neq \mu_0$): reject $H_0$ if $|Z_{calc}| > Z_{\alpha/2}$.
  • Right-tailed ($H_a: \mu > \mu_0$): reject $H_0$ if $Z_{calc} > Z_\alpha$.
  • Left-tailed ($H_a: \mu < \mu_0$): reject $H_0$ if $Z_{calc} < -Z_\alpha$.
Read the formula as a sentence "How far is my sample mean from the claim, measured in standard errors?" A Z of 3 means the sample landed three standard errors away from what the claim predicted — a long way, in a world where 95% of samples land within about two.

4.8 · Testing a mean when σ is unknown — the t test

Assumptions Population standard deviation $\sigma$ is unknown, so the sample standard deviation $S$ replaces it · the population is normally distributed (or $n \ge 30$).
t test for a mean, σ² unknown
$$ t = \frac{\bar{X} - \mu_0}{\dfrac{S}{\sqrt{n}}} \qquad \text{with } df = n-1 $$
identical in shape to the Z test — only S replaces σ, and the critical value now comes from the t table with n−1 degrees of freedom
Decision rule Compare the computed $t$ with the critical value at $df = n-1$:
  • Two-tailed: reject $H_0$ if $|t| > t_{\alpha/2,\,n-1}$.
  • Right-tailed: reject $H_0$ if $t > t_{\alpha,\,n-1}$.
  • Left-tailed: reject $H_0$ if $t < -t_{\alpha,\,n-1}$.
The one question that picks the test Was the standard deviation given as a population fact, or computed from the sample? "It is known from past records that σ = 5" → Z. "The sample had a standard deviation of 5" → t. Everything else about the two procedures is identical.

4.9 · Testing a proportion

Notes from the slides
  • Tests of the population proportion evaluate claims regarding a population proportion $P$, using the sample proportion $\hat{p} = \frac{X}{n}$, where $X$ is the number of successes in $n$ trials.
  • Conditions: the sampling distribution of $\hat{p}$ is approximately normal if $nP_0 \ge 5$ and $n(1-P_0) \ge 5$.
  • Hypotheses: $H_0: P = P_0$ versus $H_a: P \neq P_0$ (or $P > P_0$, or $P < P_0$).
Z test for a proportion
$$ Z = \frac{\hat{p} - P_0}{\sqrt{\dfrac{P_0(1-P_0)}{n}}} $$
p̂ the sample proportion · P₀ the proportion claimed in H₀ · degrees of freedom: not applicable, $Z \sim N(0,1)$
Use P₀ in the denominator, not p̂

This is the single most common slip in proportion tests, and it is the one place this chapter differs from Chapter 3.

  • Testing (this chapter): the standard error uses $P_0$ — the value from $H_0$ — because the whole calculation is done in the world where $H_0$ is true.
  • Confidence interval (Chapter 3): the standard error uses $\hat{p}$, because there is no claimed value to assume.

4.10 · Testing a variance

As in Chapter 3, questions about spread use the chi-square distribution — and again the two cases differ by whether the population mean is known.

Case I — population mean μ is known

Conditions The population is normally distributed and the population mean $\mu$ is known. Hypotheses: $H_0: \sigma^2 = \sigma_0^2$ versus $H_a: \sigma^2 \neq \sigma_0^2$ (or $>$, or $<$).
Chi-square test for a variance, μ known
$$ \chi^{2} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i - \mu)^{2}}{\sigma_0^{2}} \qquad \text{with } df = n $$
Σ(Xᵢ−μ)² squared deviations from the known mean · σ₀² the variance claimed in H₀ · df = n, since no degree of freedom is lost estimating the mean
Decision rules (df = n)
  • Right-tail ($H_a: \sigma^2 > \sigma_0^2$): reject $H_0$ if $\chi^2 > \chi^2_{n,\,\alpha}$.
  • Left-tail ($H_a: \sigma^2 < \sigma_0^2$): reject $H_0$ if $\chi^2 < \chi^2_{n,\,1-\alpha}$.
  • Two-tail ($H_a: \sigma^2 \neq \sigma_0^2$): reject $H_0$ if $\chi^2 > \chi^2_{n,\,\alpha/2}$ or $\chi^2 < \chi^2_{n,\,1-\alpha/2}$.

Case II — population mean μ is unknown

Conditions The population is normally distributed and $\mu$ is unknown, estimated by the sample mean $\bar{X}$.
Chi-square test for a variance, μ unknown
$$ \chi^{2} = \frac{(n-1)S^{2}}{\sigma_0^{2}} = \frac{\sum_{i=1}^{n}(X_i-\bar{X})^{2}}{\sigma_0^{2}} \qquad \text{with } df = n-1 $$
S² the sample variance · df = n − 1, because one degree of freedom is lost estimating μ with x̄
Decision rules (df = n − 1)
  • Right-tail: reject $H_0$ if $\chi^2_{n-1} > \chi^2_{n-1,\,\alpha}$.
  • Left-tail: reject $H_0$ if $\chi^2_{n-1} < \chi^2_{n-1,\,1-\alpha}$.
  • Two-tail: reject $H_0$ if $\chi^2_{n-1} > \chi^2_{n-1,\,\alpha/2}$ or $\chi^2_{n-1} < \chi^2_{n-1,\,1-\alpha/2}$.
Two separate lookups, again Because the chi-square curve is skewed, a two-tailed variance test needs two different critical values — you cannot use $\pm$ one number. Look up $\chi^2_{\alpha/2}$ for the right boundary and $\chi^2_{1-\alpha/2}$ for the left one, from the chi-square table supplied in your exam.

4.12 · The deck's worked example — computing α and β directly

Sections 4.4 and 4.5 defined α and β as areas under curves. This example, taken straight from your slides, computes both of them exactly from a binomial distribution instead — which makes the two error probabilities much more concrete than any picture can.

Worked example 1 — the biased coin (Type I and Type II error)

To test whether a coin is unbiased, it is tossed 5 times. The null hypothesis of unbiasedness is rejected if and only if more than 4 heads are obtained. Find the (i) critical region, (ii) probability of Type I error, (iii) probability of Type II error, when the corresponding probability of getting a head is 0.2.

Given n = 5 tosses · reject H₀ only if more than 4 heads · under H₀ the coin is fair · under H₁ the probability of a head is 0.2
  1. State the hypothesis. The coin being unbiased means $H_0: p = \frac{1}{2}$.
  2. Model the count. Let $X$ be the number of heads in five tosses. Under $H_0$ it is binomial: $$P(X = x \mid H_0) = \binom{n}{x}p^{x}q^{n-x} = \binom{5}{x}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{x}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{5-x} = \binom{5}{x}\left(\tfrac{1}{2}\right)^{5} = \tfrac{1}{32}\binom{5}{x}$$ for $x = 0, 1, \dots, 5$.
  3. (i) The critical region. We reject $H_0$ if more than 4 heads are obtained, and the only value above 4 is 5. So the critical region is $X = 5$.
  4. (ii) Type I error. This is rejecting $H_0$ while $H_0$ is true, so compute the probability of landing in the critical region with a fair coin: $$\alpha = P(\text{Reject } H_0 \mid H_0) = P(X = 5 \mid p = \tfrac{1}{2}) = \tfrac{1}{32}\binom{5}{5} = \tfrac{1}{32} = 0.03125$$
  5. (iii) Type II error. This is failing to reject $H_0$ while $H_1$ is true, so now use $p = 0.2$ — and it is easier to compute the rejection probability and subtract: $$\beta = P(\text{Accept } H_0 \mid H_1) = 1 - P(\text{Reject } H_0 \mid H_1) = 1 - P(X = 5 \mid p = 0.2)$$ $$= 1 - \binom{5}{5}(0.2)^{5} = 1 - 0.00032 = 0.99968$$
(i) X = 5  ·  (ii) α = 0.03125  ·  (iii) β = 0.99968
Read those two numbers together — this is the lesson

α is a tiny 3.1%: this test almost never convicts a fair coin. But β is a catastrophic 99.97%: if the coin really only lands heads 20% of the time, this test will fail to notice essentially every single time.

Its power is $1 - \beta = 0.00032$. The rule "reject only on 5 heads out of 5" is so cautious that it is almost incapable of detecting anything — and note that a coin biased towards tails produces fewer heads, which this right-tailed rule can never flag at all. Driving α down to near zero pushed β to near one, exactly as section 4.5 warned.

4.13 · Practice questions

These come from the setup examples in your slide deck. Getting $H_0$ and $H_a$ right is worth doing slowly — every later step inherits the mistake.

Question 1 — stating the hypotheses

A manufacturer is filling 40 oz. packages with flour. The company wants the package contents to average 40 ounces. State the null and alternative hypotheses.

Given target fill = 40 ounces · the company cares about deviation in either direction

Hint: the equality always goes in $H_0$. Would over-filling also be a problem, or only under-filling? That decides whether $H_a$ uses $\neq$ or $<$.

  1. The claim being tested is that the process is on target, which carries the equality: $H_0: \mu = 40$ oz.
  2. The company wants to detect any departure from 40 — packages that are too full waste flour, packages that are too light short the customer. So the alternative is non-directional.
  3. $H_a: \mu \neq 40$ oz.
  4. Because $H_a$ uses $\neq$, this is a two-tailed test, and α is split between both tails.
H₀: μ = 40 oz  ·  Hₐ: μ ≠ 40 oz  (two-tailed)
Question 2 — parameter, not statistic

The average number of TV sets in U.S. homes is claimed to be equal to three.

a) State $H_0$ and $H_a$ for testing this claim.
b) Your classmate writes $H_0: \bar{X} = 3$. Explain why that is wrong.

Given the claimed average is 3 TV sets per home

Hint for (b): which of $\mu$ and $\bar{X}$ do you already know the value of once you have taken your sample?

  1. (a) The claim carries the equality, so $H_0: \mu = 3$, and its opposite is $H_1: \mu \neq 3$ — the average number of TV sets is not equal to three.
  2. (b) A hypothesis is always a statement about a population parameter, never about a sample statistic.
  3. $\bar{X}$ is computed directly from your own sample — you can simply look at it, so there is nothing to test and nothing uncertain about it. If your sample gave $\bar{X} = 2.8$, then $\bar{X} = 2.8$ is a fact, not a hypothesis.
  4. $\mu$ is the unknown quantity out in the population. That is the only thing a test can be about.
(a) H₀: μ = 3 · H₁: μ ≠ 3  ·  (b) hypotheses are about the unknown parameter μ, never the observed statistic X̄
Question 3 — one-tailed or two-tailed?

For each pair of hypotheses below, say whether the test is one-tailed or two-tailed, and if one-tailed, which tail holds the rejection region.

a)  $H_0: \mu \ge 40$  versus  $H_a: \mu < 40$
b)  $H_0: P \le 0.18$  versus  $H_a: P > 0.18$
c)  $H_0: \mu = 12$  versus  $H_a: \mu \neq 12$

Hint: look only at the sign in $H_a$. It points at the rejection region.

  1. (a) $H_a$ uses $<$, a direction, so it is one-tailed — specifically left-tailed. Reject $H_0$ if the test statistic falls below $-Z_\alpha$.
  2. (b) $H_a$ uses $>$, so it is one-tailed, right-tailed. Reject $H_0$ if the statistic exceeds $+Z_\alpha$. Note this one is about a proportion, so the statistic is $Z = \frac{\hat{p}-P_0}{\sqrt{P_0(1-P_0)/n}}$ with $P_0 = 0.18$.
  3. (c) $H_a$ uses $\neq$, no direction, so it is two-tailed. Reject if $|Z| > Z_{\alpha/2}$ — and remember α is split, so at 5% the critical value is 1.96, not 1.645.
  4. Notice in (a) and (b) that $H_0$ carries $\ge$ and $\le$: the equality is still on the null side, exactly as the rule requires.
(a) one-tailed, left  ·  (b) one-tailed, right  ·  (c) two-tailed
Question 4 — the logic of rejection

A claim states that the population mean age is 50, so $H_0: \mu = 50$. A random sample is selected and the sample mean age turns out to be $\bar{X} = 20$. Explain, in the language of sampling distributions, why this leads us to reject $H_0$.

Given $H_0: \mu = 50$ · observed $\bar{X} = 20$

Hint: assume H₀ is true, then ask where 20 sits on the sampling distribution of x̄ centred at 50.

  1. Assume $H_0$ is true. Then the sampling distribution of $\bar{X}$ is centred at $\mu = 50$, with spread $\sigma/\sqrt{n}$.
  2. Locate the observation. A sample mean of 20 sits far out in the left tail of that distribution.
  3. Ask the key question. If the population mean really were 50, how likely is it that a random sample would average only 20? Very unlikely — that value lies in the rejection region, where the probability of occurrence under $H_0$ is $\le \alpha$.
  4. Draw the conclusion. Since it is unlikely we would get a sample mean of this value if in fact 50 were the population mean, we reject the null hypothesis that $\mu = 50$.
  5. Note the form of the argument: we never proved $\mu \neq 50$ directly. We showed that assuming $\mu = 50$ makes our data absurd — proof by contradiction, run on probabilities.
Reject H₀: a sample mean of 20 is too improbable under a distribution centred at 50

Chapter recap

  • $H_0$ holds the equality ($=, \le, \ge$); $H_a$ never does ($\neq, >, <$). Both are about parameters, never about $\bar{x}$.
  • Reject or fail to reject — never "accept $H_0$".
  • The direction of $H_a$ sets the tails, and a two-tailed test splits α in half (1.96, not 1.645, at 5%).
  • Type I (α) is a false alarm; Type II (β) is a miss. They trade off at fixed $n$; only a bigger sample improves both. Power $= 1-\beta$.
  • p-value: if p < α, reject. Double it for a two-tailed test. It is not the probability that $H_0$ is true.
  • Pick the statistic from one question: mean with σ known → Z; mean with σ unknown → t (df $= n-1$); proportion → Z with $P_0$ inside the standard error; variance → χ² (df $= n$ if μ known, $n-1$ if not).
  • Always finish in words about the real situation — step 6 is worth marks on its own.
